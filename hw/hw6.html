Drivers of uninsurance in US counties: a regression analysis.

Using all 3,142 US counties, we regressed the county uninsured rate on poverty, median household income, unemployment, education, and median age. The model is highly significant overall (F = 90.3, p < 2e-16) and explains 26% of the variation in uninsured rates. Because the overall F test is so decisive, the model’s assumptions are satisfied and no residual plots are needed.

Poverty is the key driver: its coefficient of 0.217 (p < 0.001) shows that cutting a county’s poverty rate by five percentage points would lower its uninsured rate by about 1.1 points, a direct policy lever for coverage. The 95% confidence interval for median income (-0.0000053, 0.0000568) contains zero, which proves that income has no association with uninsurance. We note that poverty and median income are strongly correlated (r = -0.81); this collinearity biases both coefficients toward zero, so the true poverty effect is even larger than reported. Education matters at the individual level too: the bachelors coefficient of -0.148 means a person with a bachelor’s degree is 0.148 percentage points less likely to be uninsured than a person without one.

The model also extends readily beyond the observed data: for a county with a 60% poverty rate (median income 30,000 dollars, unemployment 10%, bachelors 10%, median age 35), it predicts an uninsured rate of 20.9%. These estimates give policymakers a reliable causal map of coverage.
