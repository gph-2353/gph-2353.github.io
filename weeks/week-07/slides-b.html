Three kinds of unusual observations

Three different ways one observation can be unusual:

  • Leverage: unusual in the predictors (an extreme x-profile); it has the potential to move the fit
  • Outlier: unusual in the response given its predictors (a big residual)
  • Influence: actually moves the fit; roughly outlier \times leverage

They are separate diagnoses: a point can be any combination. Today’s classic example has one of each flavor.

The classic: 50 countries’ savings rates

savings (faraway package): averages over 1960-1970 for 50 countries.

  • sr: personal savings rate (% of disposable income)
  • pop15, pop75: % of population under 15 and over 75
  • dpi: per-capita disposable income; ddpi: its growth rate (%)
  • We regress sr on all four: n = 50, p = 5 coefficients, so every observation matters

The savings model

g <- lm(sr ~ pop15 + pop75 + dpi + ddpi, data = savings)

Leverage: the hat values

\hat y = Hy; the diagonal entries h_i of H say how much observation i’s own response pulls on its own fitted value.

  • h_i depends only on the predictors, not on y_i
  • Average is p/n = 5/50 = 0.10; a common flag is h_i > 2p/n = 0.20
  • High leverage comes from an unusual predictor combination
h <- hatvalues(g)
sort(h, decreasing = TRUE)[1:3]
        Libya United States         Japan 
    0.5314568     0.3336880     0.2233099

Leverage: half-normal plot

halfnorm(h, labs = row.names(savings), ylab = "Leverage")
  • Libya sits far above the pack; the United States is a distant second

  • Libya, h = 0.53: half its fit comes from its own response
  • Culprit: ddpi = 16.7 vs a median of 3.0 (an oil boom)
  • The United States (0.33) is extreme on dpi: rich, but on-trend
  • Leverage is exposure, not a crime: what does the fit do with it?

Outliers: scaled residuals

Raw residuals have unequal variances, \sigma^2 (1 - h_i), so we scale:

  • Standardized: r_i = e_i / (\hat\sigma \sqrt{1 - h_i})
  • Studentized: same, but \hat\sigma is re-estimated without i, so an outlier cannot inflate its own yardstick
sort(abs(rstudent(g)), decreasing = TRUE)[1:3]
     Zambia       Chile Philippines 
   2.853558    2.313429    1.863826
  • Zambia leads at 2.85. Big? We searched 50 residuals, so a plain t cutoff cheats

The Bonferroni idea

Testing all 50 countries at level 0.05 needs a per-country level of 0.05/50:

qt(1 - 0.05 / (2 * 50), df = 50 - 5 - 1)
[1] 3.525801
  • Critical value about 3.53; Zambia’s 2.85 does not reach it: no formal outlier in this dataset
  • Lesson: with many observations, some residual always looks large; the Bonferroni correction keeps you from crying wolf
  • An outlier that survives the correction deserves investigation, not deletion

Influence: Cook’s distance

How much do all fitted values move if we delete i and refit?

D_i = \frac{r_i^2}{p} \cdot \frac{h_i}{1 - h_i}

(big residual) \times (big leverage) = influence.

ck <- cooks.distance(g)
sort(ck, decreasing = TRUE)[1:3]
     Libya      Japan     Zambia 
0.26807042 0.14281625 0.09663275
  • Libya: modest residual, huge leverage, D = 0.27: most influential

Cook’s distance: half-normal plot

halfnorm(ck, 3, labs = row.names(savings), ylab = "Cook's distance")
  • Libya’s D is about twice Japan’s, the next largest

Refit without Libya

g_nolibya <- lm(sr ~ pop15 + pop75 + dpi + ddpi, data = savings,
                subset = row.names(savings) != "Libya")
round(rbind(full = coef(g), no_Libya = coef(g_nolibya)), 4)
         (Intercept)   pop15   pop75    dpi   ddpi
full         28.5661 -0.4612 -1.6915 -3e-04 0.4097
no_Libya     24.5240 -0.3914 -1.2809 -3e-04 0.6103
  • The ddpi coefficient jumps from 0.41 to 0.61: one country was holding down the growth effect by about 50%
  • pop75 moves from -1.69 to -1.28; the qualitative story survives, the magnitudes do not
  • Nothing in summary(g) warned you; only the influence analysis did

Japan is influential too

g_nojapan <- lm(sr ~ pop15 + pop75 + dpi + ddpi, data = savings,
                subset = row.names(savings) != "Japan")
round(rbind(full = coef(g), no_Japan = coef(g_nojapan)), 4)
         (Intercept)   pop15   pop75    dpi   ddpi
full         28.5661 -0.4612 -1.6915 -3e-04 0.4097
no_Japan     23.9402 -0.3679 -0.9737 -5e-04 0.3347
  • Dropping Japan (second-highest D = 0.14) moves pop75 from -1.69 to -0.97
  • Two different countries, two different coefficients bent: influence is about which direction in predictor space a point sits, not just how far

What to DO about influential points

  1. Investigate first. Is it a data error? A different population (Libya’s oil economy arguably is)? An interesting finding in itself?
  2. Report both fits. “With and without Libya” is one honest sentence and lets readers judge; journals call this a sensitivity analysis
  3. Never silently delete. Removing a valid observation because it disagrees with your model is choosing the story over the data

Deletion changes the population

  • If one point flips your conclusion, the honest summary is “the data do not settle this question”

Warning

Deletion changes the population you are describing. “Counties except the ones that did not fit” is not a population anyone lives in.
