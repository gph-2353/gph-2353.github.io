This week’s question

You have seven candidate predictors of life expectancy. Which ones earn a place in the model?

  • Until now, someone (me) handed you the predictor list
  • Your project will not come with one; neither will your job
  • Today: principled ways to choose, and the ways choosing can fool you

The running example is a classic: US states in the 1970s, predicting life expectancy from state.x77. Old data, but the canonical selection example, and a genuinely public health outcome.

Why select at all?

Why not just keep every variable you have?

  • Parsimony: a 4-variable model can be explained to a health department; a 40-variable model cannot
  • Precision: every unneeded predictor spends a degree of freedom and inflates the standard errors of the coefficients you care about
  • Prediction: extra noise variables make out-of-sample predictions worse, not better
  • Occam: when two models explain the data about equally well, prefer the simpler one

What selection is not

What selection is not

Selection cannot tell you which variables cause the outcome. It finds a compact summary of association, nothing more.

  • A dropped variable may still matter causally; a kept one may only be a proxy

Reading the full model

  • Murder rate: strongly negative (t = -6.5), the dominant predictor
  • Income, Illiteracy, Area: p-values above 0.9. Useless here? Or masked by correlated predictors?
  • R^2 = 0.736, adjusted R^2 = 0.692: seven predictors, but plenty of overlap among them

Temptation: delete everything with a big p-value, all at once. Resist it. When predictors correlate, each p-value is computed given all the others are in the model; remove one and every other p-value changes.

Testing-based backward elimination

The oldest recipe:

  1. Fit the full model
  2. Drop the predictor with the largest p-value above some cutoff
  3. Refit; repeat until everything remaining is below the cutoff
  • Forward selection: start empty, add the most significant candidate, repeat
  • Stepwise: alternate; variables may enter, leave, and re-enter

Problems with testing-based selection

  • One at a time only. After each removal the whole table changes; the order you test in changes where you land
  • Inflated significance. You ran many implicit tests to get here; the surviving p-values look better than they should
  • The cutoff is arbitrary. 0.05 answers a testing question, not a “best model” question; a variable can be a useful predictor without clearing it
  • Forward and backward need not agree, and neither is guaranteed to find the best subset

Criterion-based selection

Modern practice: keep the search (add or drop one term at a time), but replace repeated testing with a criterion scoring whole models.

All three reward fit and charge for complexity, at different prices. For a model with p parameters fit to n observations:

\begin{aligned} \mathrm{AIC} &= n\log(\mathrm{RSS}/n) + 2p \\ \mathrm{BIC} &= n\log(\mathrm{RSS}/n) + p\log n \end{aligned}

  • Smaller AIC or BIC is better; comparisons are only meaningful on the same rows of data

AIC, BIC, and adjusted R-squared

  • AIC: price 2 per parameter; aims at good prediction; keeps borderline helpers
  • BIC: price \log n per parameter (here \log 50 \approx 3.9); aims at recovering the true small model; more ruthless as n grows
  • Adjusted R^2: 1 - \frac{\mathrm{RSS}/(n-p)}{\mathrm{TSS}/(n-1)}; gentlest penalty of the three; fine for describing fit, weakest as a selector

The selected model, in words

Life expectancy ~ Population + Murder + HS graduation + Frost:

  • Each additional murder per 100,000 population is associated with about 0.30 years lower life expectancy, adjusting for the other three
  • R^2 = 0.736: essentially the full model’s fit (0.7362) with three fewer predictors
  • Adjusted R^2 rose from 0.692 to 0.713: the deleted trio cost fit less than they cost degrees of freedom

Population survived with p = 0.052: AIC is not a significance test, and it keeps predictors that help prediction even near the testing cutoff.
