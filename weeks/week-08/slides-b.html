When a straight line is not enough

  • Skew and curves strain a line: huge values dominate
  • Today: reshape variables so a line fits well again

Transforming the response: log(y)

\log(y_i) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{i1} + \cdots + \varepsilon_i \Longleftrightarrow\quad y_i = e^{\beta_0} \cdot e^{\beta_1 x_{i1}} \cdots \cdot e^{\varepsilon_i}

  • Additive on the log scale = multiplicative on the original scale
  • A one-unit increase in x_1 multiplies the typical y by e^{\beta_1}
  • For small coefficients, e^{\beta} - 1 \approx \beta: a coefficient of 0.016 means about a 1.6% increase per unit of x

What log does to the outcome

Histograms of uninsured on the raw and log scales:

hist_y <- cty |>
  transmute(`uninsured (%)` = uninsured,
            `log(uninsured)` = log(uninsured)) |>
  pivot_longer(everything(), names_to = "scale", values_to = "value") |>
  mutate(scale = fct_inorder(scale)) |>
  ggplot(aes(value)) +
  geom_histogram(bins = 40, fill = "grey40", color = "white") +
  facet_wrap(~scale, scales = "free_x") +
  labs(x = NULL, y = "Counties")

What log does to the outcome

  • Raw scale: right-skewed, with a long tail past 20%
  • Log scale: roughly symmetric; the long tail is pulled in

Reading a log-y model

m_log <- lm(log(uninsured) ~ poverty + bachelors + median_age, data = cty)
round(coef(m_log), 4)
(Intercept)     poverty   bachelors  median_age 
     3.0849      0.0157     -0.0155     -0.0210 
round(100 * (exp(coef(m_log)[-1]) - 1), 1)
   poverty  bachelors median_age 
       1.6       -1.5       -2.1
  • “Adjusting for education and median age, each additional percentage point of poverty is associated with about 1.6% higher uninsured rate (multiplicatively), among US counties in 2019”
  • Careful: that is 1.6 percent of the rate (8% becomes 8.13%), not 1.6 percentage points

When does log(y) help?

  • Right skew in y: log pulls in the long tail
  • Multiplicative structure: effects that plausibly act in percent terms (incomes, rates, concentrations, counts of anything that grows)
  • Variance rising with the mean: the funnel-shaped residual plot from Week 6; log often stabilizes it
  • Here the gain is modest and honest: R^2 moves from 0.262 (raw) to 0.272 (log), but the residuals lose most of their right skew. Transformations are for validity, not for chasing R^2

Box-Cox: a formal guide

Box-Cox considers the family y^{(\lambda)} = (y^\lambda - 1)/\lambda, with \lambda = 0 meaning \log y, and profiles the likelihood over \lambda:

MASS::boxcox(lm(uninsured ~ poverty + bachelors + median_age, data = cty),
             lambda = seq(-0.5, 1, 0.05))

The Box-Cox profile

  • Dotted lines: the peak and its 95% interval, which covers 0 but not 1

Choosing lambda pragmatically

For our model the likelihood peaks at \hat\lambda \approx 0.07 with a 95% interval of roughly (-0.03, 0.16).

  • Nobody can interpret y^{0.07}. The interval covers 0, so use the log
  • Snap to interpretable values: 1 (nothing), 1/2 (square root), 0 (log), -1 (reciprocal)
  • If the interval covers 1, resist transforming at all: interpretability beats optimality
  • Box-Cox needs y > 0; it advises, it does not command

Transforming predictors: log(x)

Density spans 0.1 to almost 70,000 people per sq mi: a linear term lets NYC set the slope for rural Kansas.

den <- county_health |> drop_na(uninsured, density) |> filter(density > 0)
m_raw <- lm(uninsured ~ density, data = den)
m_logx <- lm(uninsured ~ log(density), data = den)
c(r2_raw = summary(m_raw)$r.squared, r2_logx = summary(m_logx)$r.squared)
     r2_raw     r2_logx 
0.002217547 0.054179990
  • R^2 jumps from 0.002 to 0.054: the raw scale saw almost nothing
  • Coefficient -0.67: each doubling of density goes with about a -0.47 point change in uninsured rate (coefficient \times \log 2)

What log does to the predictor

  • Raw scale: nearly every county sits in the first bar
  • Log scale: a roughly symmetric hump centered near 4

Seeing why log(x) wins

Code 
den |>
  mutate(`Raw density` = density, `log(density)` = log(density)) |>
  pivot_longer(c(`Raw density`, `log(density)`), names_to = "scale") |>
  mutate(scale = fct_relevel(scale, "Raw density")) |>
  ggplot(aes(value, uninsured)) +
  geom_point(alpha = 0.2) +
  geom_smooth(method = "lm") +
  facet_wrap(~scale, scales = "free_x") +
  labs(x = NULL, y = "Uninsured (%)")
  • Log spreads counties out of the raw-scale sliver, so all inform the fit

Polynomials: curvature without logs

lm(uninsured ~ poly(median_age, 2), data = county_health)
  • Adds curvature while keeping y and x on their original scales
  • Always keep lower-order terms when you include higher ones
  • Danger zone: polynomial tails do absurd things outside the data range; never extrapolate a quadratic
  • Rule of thumb: log for skew and multiplicative stories, polynomials for genuine humps and dips

Cautions before you transform

  • Back-transformation: a log-y prediction e^{\hat y} estimates the median of y, not the mean; say which you report
  • Zeros: \log 0 = -\infty. One county has density 0, one has uninsured 0. Dropping a known handful is honest if disclosed; \log(y + 1) changes the question and depends on units
  • Interpretation debt: pay it back in the write-up; if you cannot explain the coefficient to a health department, reconsider
  • Transform to fix structure (skew, curvature, funnels), not to nudge a p-value
