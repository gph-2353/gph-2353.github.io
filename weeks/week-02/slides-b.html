Fitted values and residuals

Every observation splits into what the model explains and what it misses:

y_i = \underbrace{\hat y_i}_{\text{fitted}} + \underbrace{e_i}_{\text{residual}}

bp_aug <- bp |>
  mutate(fitted = fitted(fit), resid = resid(fit))
bp_aug |> select(age, bp_sys, fitted, resid) |> head(4)
  age bp_sys   fitted      resid
1  43    103 119.8778 -16.877847
2  80     97 136.5618 -39.561816
3  34    107 115.8196  -8.819584
4  80    121 136.5618 -15.561816
  • Residuals always sum to zero for a least squares line with an intercept
  • The residuals are our window into everything the model gets wrong (Weeks 6-7 live here)

Decomposing variation

\underbrace{\sum (y_i - \bar y)^2}_{\text{TSS: total}} = \underbrace{\sum (\hat y_i - \bar y)^2}_{\text{explained}} + \underbrace{\sum e_i^2}_{\text{RSS: residual}}

tss <- sum((bp$bp_sys - mean(bp$bp_sys))^2)
rss <- sum(resid(fit)^2)
c(TSS = tss, RSS = rss, explained = tss - rss)
      TSS       RSS explained 
 654131.5  522138.8  131992.7

Why the decomposition works

Add and subtract the fitted value, then square and sum:

y_i - \bar y = (\hat y_i - \bar y) + e_i, \qquad e_i = y_i - \hat y_i

\sum (y_i - \bar y)^2 = \sum (\hat y_i - \bar y)^2 + \sum e_i^2 + 2 \underbrace{\sum (\hat y_i - \bar y) e_i}_{\text{cross term}}

The identity holds exactly when the cross term is zero. That is where least squares enters: it is a property of this line, not of any line.

The normal equations

Least squares minimizes S(b_0, b_1) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - b_0 - b_1 x_i)^2. Set both partial derivatives to zero:

\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial S}{\partial b_0} &= -2 \sum (y_i - b_0 - b_1 x_i) = 0 \\[2pt] \frac{\partial S}{\partial b_1} &= -2 \sum x_i (y_i - b_0 - b_1 x_i) = 0 \end{aligned}

At the minimizers \hat\beta_0, \hat\beta_1 the parenthesis is the residual e_i, giving the normal equations \sum e_i = 0 and \sum x_i e_i = 0.

The cross term vanishes

Since \hat y_i = \hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_1 x_i, split the sum:

\sum (\hat y_i - \bar y) e_i = \hat\beta_0 \underbrace{\sum e_i}_{0} + \hat\beta_1 \underbrace{\sum x_i e_i}_{0} - \bar y \underbrace{\sum e_i}_{0} = 0

Substituting back:

\underbrace{\sum (y_i - \bar y)^2}_{\text{TSS}} = \underbrace{\sum (\hat y_i - \bar y)^2}_{\text{explained}} + \underbrace{\sum e_i^2}_{\text{RSS}} \qquad \blacksquare

Checking it numerically

e <- resid(fit)
ess <- sum((fitted(fit) - mean(bp$bp_sys))^2)
c(sum_e      = sum(e),
  sum_xe     = sum(bp$age * e),
  cross_term = sum((fitted(fit) - mean(bp$bp_sys)) * e),
  tss_gap    = tss - (ess + rss))
        sum_e        sum_xe    cross_term       tss_gap 
 1.040945e-12  1.928129e-10 -2.877698e-11 -4.656613e-10

R-squared

R^2 = 1 - \frac{RSS}{TSS} = \text{fraction of variation in } y \text{ captured by the line}

c(by_hand = 1 - rss / tss,
  from_summary = summary(fit)$r.squared,
  cor_squared = cor(bp$bp_sys, bp$age)^2)
     by_hand from_summary  cor_squared 
   0.2017832    0.2017832    0.2017832
  • In simple regression, R^2 = r^2 exactly
  • Age explains about 21% of BP variation: a strong result for one variable in individual-level health data

What is a “good” R-squared?

  • Physics lab: 0.99. County averages: 0.6-0.8. Individual humans: 0.05-0.3
  • Individual outcomes are dominated by unmeasured biology and behavior; that is reality, not a failed model
  • A tiny R^2 can still be a precise, important association (small effects on millions of people matter in public health)

Warning

R^2 never tells you whether the model is correct, causal, or useful for prediction of individuals. It is one number, not a verdict.

The residual standard error

sigma(fit)
[1] 15.82107
  • Typical miss: about 16 mmHg
  • Often more honest than R^2: it is in the units you care about
  • “Knowing age narrows our BP guess to roughly plus or minus 15 mmHg” means something to a clinician
